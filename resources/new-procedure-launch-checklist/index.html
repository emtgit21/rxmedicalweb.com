You trained in a new procedure: a first-month marketing checklist

An ordered checklist for the first month after training, covering what to prepare, who to tell first, and how to know whether the new service is gaining traction.

By the Rx Medical Web team · November 13, 2026

In short

  • Get consent forms, scheduling and a front desk script in place before you promote the new procedure.
  • Build one dedicated service page, reviewed by a physician, so every email, listing and ad has a place to send people.
  • Tell existing patients first, because they already trust you and let you test the workflow before you pay for ads.
  • Track inquiries, sources, consultations and procedures from the first week so you can fix the weakest step before spending more.

You finished the course, the supplies are on order, and you are ready to treat. The most common mistake at this point is to buy ads before the practice can explain, book and document the new service. The order below works for most procedures, whether you trained in aesthetics, weight loss, hormone therapy, regenerative medicine or pain management, at Empire Medical Training or elsewhere. Work through it in sequence and adjust the pace to your schedule.

Start with the paperwork and the people, not the promotion

Before any patient hears about the new service, make sure the practice can deliver it properly.

  1. Consent forms. Have a procedure-specific consent form and written aftercare instructions ready. Templates from a course are a starting point. Ask your counsel and your malpractice carrier to review the final versions, and confirm that your coverage includes the procedure.
  2. Scope and delegation. Rules on who may perform or assist with a procedure, and how it may be advertised, vary by state and by license. Confirm the details with your counsel or compliance contact before you publish anything.
  3. Scheduling. Create the appointment type, set its length, and decide whether a consultation comes first.
  4. Front desk script. Write a short script that covers what the service is in plain words, who performs it, what a first visit involves, what it costs or how pricing is discussed, and how to book. Add one firm rule: staff do not answer clinical questions. Those go to the physician or to a consultation.

Build one service page that answers real questions

Every later step points somewhere, and that place should be a dedicated page on your own website, not a line in a list of services. A useful page includes:

  • What the procedure is and what an appointment looks like, step by step.
  • Who performs it, with your training and credentials stated accurately.
  • What a patient should expect before and after, in general terms, with a clear note that suitability is decided at a consultation.
  • The questions your front desk already hears, with honest answers.
  • One obvious way to book: a phone number and a short form.

Keep the language factual and avoid promising outcomes. AI can help draft the page, but a physician should review every clinical statement before it goes live. If the form collects anything about a patient's health, make sure it is handled with HIPAA requirements in mind.

Define a simple offer and tell existing patients first

An offer does not have to be a discount. A consultation with the physician, an introductory package or an educational evening at the office all give people a reason to act now. Choose one, write it in a sentence, and have your compliance contact confirm that it fits your state's advertising rules and any payer rules that apply.

Then start with the people who already trust you. Existing patients are the least expensive audience to reach and the most likely to book early.

  • Send an email to your patient list, within the communication preferences patients have given you.
  • Put a sign at the front desk and a card in the exam rooms.
  • Mention it during visits when it is relevant to the patient in front of you.
  • Update your Google Business Profile and other listings so the service appears there.

These first patients also give you practice at the whole workflow, from the phone call to the follow-up, before strangers arrive from an ad.

Collect photos and reviews the right way

For visual procedures, photos are often what persuades a new patient, and they take time to accumulate. Set the process up on day one.

  • Use the same lighting, background and angles for every before and after image.
  • Get written authorization that specifically covers marketing use before any photo is published, and store it with the record. Your counsel can confirm the wording.
  • Show your own patients only, without retouching, and avoid implying that one person's outcome is typical.

For reviews, ask every patient the same way, shortly after the visit, with a direct link. Do not offer anything in exchange, and do not ask only the happy ones. When you reply to a review, keep it general and never confirm that the writer is a patient or discuss their care.

Add advertising once the basics work

When the page is live, the phones are answered well and a few patients have gone through the process, paid advertising has something solid to send people to. Start small and narrow.

  • Search ads reach people already looking for the procedure in your area. Send them to the service page, not the home page.
  • Social ads build awareness, but health and before and after content is restricted on most platforms. Read the current policies before you design anything.
  • Be careful with tracking tools on pages where patients submit health information. This is an area regulators watch, so review the setup with your compliance contact.

Measure a few things from the first week

You do not need a dashboard. A shared sheet that the front desk updates is enough.

  • Inquiries about the new service, by phone and by form.
  • How each person heard about it. Ask every caller.
  • Consultations booked, consultations attended and procedures performed.
  • What you spent on promotion.

At the end of the month, look at where inquiries drop off. If people call but do not book, the script or the pricing conversation needs work. If they visit the page but do not call, the page does. Fix the weakest step before you spend more.
