What a medical practice website needs in 2026

A practical checklist for physicians and practice managers covering speed, patient questions, booking, credentials, reviews, accessibility, structured data, physician review and measurement.

By the Rx Medical Web team · November 13, 2026

In short

  • Design the site for phones first and remove anything that slows it down.
  • Answer the questions your front desk hears most often, and keep calling and booking one tap away on every page.
  • Show accurate physician credentials and recent reviews, and have a physician review any medical content that AI helps draft.
  • Measure calls and completed bookings instead of visits, and handle anything that touches patient data with HIPAA requirements in mind.

A patient who lands on your website usually has a phone in hand and a specific question in mind. The site either answers it and makes the next step easy, or the patient goes back to the search results. Here is what we think a practice website needs to do well this year, in rough order of priority.

Build for the phone first and keep it fast

Many patients will see your site on a phone, often over a cellular connection. Design the phone version first and treat the desktop version as the adaptation.

  • Compress images, and avoid autoplay video and heavy sliders at the top of the page.
  • Limit third-party scripts. Every chat widget, tracker and custom font adds load time.
  • Use text that reads without zooming and buttons large enough to tap with a thumb.
  • Test on an ordinary phone over a cellular connection, not on the office wifi.

Answer the questions your front desk hears every day

Your staff already know what patients want to know. Ask them to write down what callers ask over a few days, then check that the site answers each question clearly.

  • Which conditions you see and which treatments you offer, named in plain words.
  • Which insurance plans you accept, and how payment works for elective services.
  • Location, parking, hours and how to reach the practice after hours.
  • What happens at a first visit and what to bring.
  • How to request refills, records and forms.

Give each main service its own page. Describe what the service is, who typically asks about it and what a visit involves. Do not promise outcomes. Advertising rules for medical services vary by state and specialty, so confirm the wording with your counsel or compliance contact.

Put booking and contact one tap away

The phone number should dial when tapped, and a booking button should appear on every page and stay visible as the patient scrolls. If you offer online scheduling, send patients straight to available times and not to a request form that waits for a reply. Keep forms short and ask only for what you need to schedule.

Any form, chat tool or scheduling system that collects patient information should be chosen and configured with HIPAA requirements in mind. That includes the analytics and advertising tools running on those pages. Ask your compliance contact to review the vendors and the agreements you have with them.

If you add an AI assistant to the site, limit it to practical matters such as hours, directions and scheduling. An AI agent should never give medical advice, and it should hand the conversation to your staff whenever a clinical question comes up.

Show who the physicians are and what patients say

Give each physician a page with a real photo, training, board certification, the procedures they perform, the languages they speak and their professional memberships. State credentials exactly as they were issued. Patients considering services such as aesthetics, weight loss or hormone therapy want to know who will treat them and how that person was trained.

Display recent reviews and link to the profiles where they were posted, so patients can read them in full. Ask for reviews consistently, not in bursts. Reply with care, because a response should not confirm that someone is a patient or mention any detail of their care. Rules on testimonials and review requests differ by state and profession, so confirm your process with counsel.

Make the site accessible to people and readable by machines

Accessibility means patients with visual, hearing or motor limitations can use the site. The basics are:

  • Strong contrast between text and background.
  • Text descriptions for images.
  • Forms with clear labels that work with a keyboard and a screen reader.
  • Captions on video.

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines are the common reference, and healthcare organizations may have legal obligations in this area. Confirm what applies to your practice with counsel.

Structured data is code that describes your practice, physicians, locations, hours and services in a standard format that search engines and AI assistants can read. It does not guarantee placement anywhere, but it lowers the chance that those systems show wrong information about you. Keep the practice name, address and phone number identical on the site and on every listing.

Have physicians review content and measure calls and bookings

AI can draft and assist with content, but a physician should review every medical page before it is published. Show the reviewer's name and the date of the last review on the page, and revisit pages when your services change.

Then measure what matters to the practice:

  1. Track phone calls that start from the website.
  2. Count completed bookings and form submissions, not only visits.
  3. Note which pages and sources produce those calls and bookings.
  4. Compare the numbers with your front desk every month to see how many became new patients.

Set up this tracking with HIPAA requirements in mind as well.

None of this requires starting over. Check your site against this list and fix the largest gaps first. At Rx Medical Web, we use this same list when we build and review websites for medical practices.
