How to ask every patient for a review, the right way

A simple, fair routine for requesting reviews from every patient, replying without revealing patient information, and staying within what review sites allow.

By the Rx Medical Web team · November 13, 2026

In short

  • Send the same review request to every patient after a visit, because selecting only satisfied patients is generally prohibited by review sites.
  • Keep text and email requests generic, handle them with HIPAA requirements in mind, and confirm consent and vendor arrangements with your counsel or compliance contact.
  • Never offer discounts, gifts, or any other incentive for a review, and ask for honest feedback instead of a positive rating.
  • Reply to reviews in general terms that do not confirm the reviewer is a patient, and move any negative conversation to a private channel.

Most practices get reviews by accident. A delighted patient writes one, an upset patient writes one, and everyone in the middle says nothing. A simple routine that asks every patient the same way, at the same point in the visit cycle, gives a more accurate picture of your practice and keeps you inside the rules that review sites set. Here is how we suggest building it.

Ask every patient, not only the happy ones

The fairest routine is also the easiest to run: every patient who completes a visit gets the same request. Staff do not have to judge who seemed pleased, and nobody has to remember to ask.

It is also what the major review sites expect. Their policies generally prohibit selecting only satisfied patients for a request, a practice often called review gating. That includes sending a satisfaction survey first and then showing the public review link only to people who answered favorably. Consumer protection rules in the United States also address how reviews are collected and presented. Read the current policies of each site you use, and confirm your approach with your counsel or compliance contact.

Asking everyone tends to produce a mix of ratings. That is fine. A profile with a steady flow of recent, varied, detailed reviews reads as more believable than a short list of perfect scores.

Send the request soon after the visit

The request works best while the visit is still fresh, typically later the same day or the next day. A few timing points are worth settling in advance:

  • Tie it to checkout. Trigger the request when the visit is closed out in your scheduling or practice management system, so it does not depend on anyone's memory.
  • Mention it in person. A short line at the front desk, such as telling patients they will receive a message asking for feedback, makes the message expected.
  • Consider the visit type. For procedures with a recovery period, such as some aesthetic treatments, you may prefer to send the request after the follow-up visit. Apply the same rule to every patient having that procedure.
  • Limit reminders. One follow-up is reasonable. Repeated messages feel like pressure.

Use text or email, and keep the message plain

Text messages are usually opened quickly, and email suits patients who prefer it or have not agreed to texts. Many practices use both, based on the communication preferences already on file.

Either way, the request is a patient communication and should be handled with HIPAA requirements in mind. Keep the message generic. It should not mention the provider's specialty if that reveals something, the reason for the visit, or any treatment. Texting has its own consent rules, so confirm with your counsel or compliance contact that your patient consents cover this kind of message and that your messaging vendor has the appropriate agreements in place.

A good request has four parts:

  1. A thank you for visiting the practice.
  2. One sentence saying that honest feedback helps other patients and helps the practice improve.
  3. A direct link to the review page, so the patient does not have to search for it.
  4. A simple way to opt out of future messages.

Ask for an honest review, not a positive one or a specific star rating.

Do not offer anything in exchange

Review sites generally do not allow incentives. That covers discounts, gift cards, free products, contest entries, and credits toward future services, whether they are offered for any review or only for a favorable one. Reviews written by staff, family members, or anyone who was not a patient are also prohibited, as is asking patients to write reviews on a device in your office when a site's policy discourages it.

The consequences can include removed reviews, a warning on your profile, or a suspended listing, and regulators may take an interest as well. The safe position is simple: the only thing a patient receives for writing a review is your thanks.

Reply without confirming anyone is a patient

Replying to reviews shows that someone at the practice is listening. The difficulty is that privacy obligations still apply even when the patient has shared details publicly. A reply should not confirm that the reviewer was seen at your practice, and it should never refer to a visit, a diagnosis, a treatment, or a bill.

  • For positive reviews: thank the person for the kind words and keep it general. Avoid phrases such as "it was great to see you" that confirm a visit.
  • For any review: speak about practice policies in general terms, such as how you aim to schedule or communicate, without discussing the individual's experience.
  • Use approved templates. Write a small set of replies, have your compliance contact review them, and assign one trained person to post them. If AI helps draft replies, a person at the practice should review each one before it is published.

Handle a negative review calmly and offline

A critical review feels personal, and the first instinct is to correct the record. Resist it. A short sequence works better:

  1. Wait until the initial frustration has passed before writing anything.
  2. Post a brief, neutral reply that states your general commitment to patient experience and invites the person to call the office manager directly. Do not confirm or dispute any details.
  3. Look into the concern internally and, if you can identify the patient, reach out privately through your normal channels.
  4. If the review violates the site's content policy, for example because it is spam or abusive, report it through the site's own process. Disagreeing with a review is usually not grounds for removal.
  5. Record what you learned. Repeated comments about wait times or billing are free operational feedback.

Do not ask the patient to remove the review in exchange for anything, and keep asking every other patient as usual. New honest reviews put a single bad one in context.
